A. Reading [28 marks]

Text 1: The Last Stall

  1. I reached the morning market before the traders. My aunt had forgotten the cash tin, and I had promised to put it under the counter before she opened. The fish crates were still stacked by the entrance. Above each stall, the lamps were dark.
  2. Near the far wall came a tapping noise: three quick knocks, then a pause. The sound travelled along the roof like a marble rolling across a table. I told myself it was a loose sign, but the air was still enough for me to hear my own breathing.
  3. Pak Arif, the caretaker, sat beside the shutter of the last stall. I asked for the key. He was reluctant to hand it over: his fingers curled around it, and he did not move until I explained why I was there. Then he said, “Your aunt said you would come.” He put the key flat on my palm rather than dropping it into my hand.
  4. The tapping stopped.
  5. As I turned the key, a corner of the shutter lifted from the inside. I stepped back. Something grey blinked at me. A narrow paw appeared, then another. The cat had been pushing a metal ladle against the shutter. When the ladle fell, it made the same three knocks before settling on the tiles.
  6. I set the tin down and opened the shutter just wide enough for the cat to slip out. “You could have warned me,” I called to Pak Arif. He took a folded cloth from his pocket. “I thought she might let you help,” he said. The cat walked straight to him and sat on the cloth. By the time my aunt arrived, I was laughing hard enough to tell her the story twice.
1. Why did the narrator go to the market early? [1]

Text 2: Start Small at Lunch

  1. Would you buy two lunches and drop one straight in the bin? Of course not. Yet at the end of a busy lunch break, it is easy to forget how much food is left behind on our trays.
  2. In a trial at one school, volunteers weighed the food left on trays from a single lunch counter. On Monday they measured 12 kilograms. On Friday, after the counter began offering a choice of portion sizes, they measured 8 kilograms. Two measurements do not prove the change will work every day, but the result gives us a reason to try it ourselves.
  3. The volunteers tried another idea, too. They moved the menu board to the beginning of the queue so students could read it before reaching the counter. If you know what is available, you can choose what you are likely to finish. Nobody should have to make that decision while a long line is waiting behind them.
  4. Start with a smaller helping if you are not sure. Ask for more if you are still hungry. And the next time you discover a meal you enjoy, tell your friends. An empty bin is a promise we can keep, one tray at a time.

The school and measurements in this practice passage are fictional.

C. Persuasive writing [24 marks]

  • Explain why the change matters and suggest at least two realistic actions.
  • Give an example that supports one of your reasons. You do not need to invent statistics.
  • Use a title, headings or short sections, persuasive language suited to classmates, and a clear call to action.
  • Leave time to proofread sentence boundaries, capital letters and punctuation.
